# CHAPTER FIVE

## SUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

### 5.1 Introduction

This chapter brings together the main findings of the study and considers what they mean for the original aim of designing and implementing FamilyFunds. It draws its conclusions from the requirements, design, implementation evidence and test results presented in the preceding chapters. It does not introduce a new experiment or convert an unexecuted activity into a successful result. In particular, the conclusions about predictive analytics remain bounded by the absence of an authorised historical training dataset, while the conclusions about hosted operation and live external services remain bounded by the evidence gaps reported in Chapter Four.

The discussion is organised around the research findings, the extent to which each objective was achieved, the study's contributions and its practical limitations. It then presents recommendations for completing the outstanding validation work and for operating the implemented system responsibly. The final sections identify suitable directions for later research and disclose the role of artificial intelligence tools in supporting the project work.

### 5.2 Summary of Findings

The study began with a practical problem: informal family contribution funds are often managed through notebooks, spreadsheets and messaging threads that do not provide one consistent account of obligations, payments, expenses and balances. The literature and system review showed that existing tools tend to address only part of this problem. General payment applications move money but do not apply family contribution rules; spreadsheets are flexible but weak in access control and audit history; and cooperative platforms are usually designed for a more formal organisational setting. This supported the need for a purpose-built system that combines family-level tenancy, defined roles, deterministic payment allocation, reporting and controlled automation.

The design findings established that logical data separation cannot depend on a single interface choice or hidden identifier. FamilyFunds therefore combines the active family context, membership records, family-scoped queries, validation and policies. Roles are attached to a person's membership in a particular family rather than treated only as a global user property. This allows the same person to hold different responsibilities in different family groups without automatically carrying privileges from one context into another. The local automated evidence reported in Chapter Four supports this design at the tested boundary, although final hosted review is still required before making a wider operational claim.

The financial implementation also confirmed the value of separating an obligation from the receipt used to settle it. Monthly contributions record what a member is expected to pay, while payment batches and allocation lines record what was received and how it was applied. The oldest-balance-first rule gives partial and lump-sum payments a repeatable outcome: earlier outstanding periods are settled before later ones, subject to the defined advance-payment limit. Transaction boundaries, row locking, idempotency controls and explicit reversal records protect the ledger from duplicate or conflicting operations. This directly answers the third research question within the local automated scope.

The broader application integrates contribution generation, manual and Paystack-related payment handling, expenses, fund adjustments, reminders, reports, receipts, scheduled outputs, account security and subscription controls. Reconciliation emerged during implementation as a useful post-design extension. It provides a controlled means of comparing internal records with imported bank transactions without pretending that this feature belonged to the original functional baseline. This distinction is important because it presents the development history as it occurred and shows how iterative software work can reveal an audit requirement that was not fully visible at the design stage.

The controlled AI work produced a governed implementation rather than an unrestricted financial chatbot. Tools obtain current family data through the application, their availability depends on the user's role, and write actions require a preview followed by explicit confirmation. These controls reduce the risk that generated language will bypass established permissions or silently alter financial records. However, the study did not execute the planned live provider acceptance matrix. The evidence therefore supports the architecture and automated safeguards, but it does not establish the factual quality, clarity or latency of live model responses. The fourth research question is consequently only partially answered.

The exploratory predictive work produced a software prototype and a protocol for future evaluation. It includes chronological splitting, leakage checks, logistic-regression code, baseline methods and controls for installing and using a model. Tests exercised these mechanisms using controlled fixtures. However, no authorised historical dataset was obtained, so no model was trained or activated for practical use and no accuracy, F1, AUC or calibration result is claimed. The revised fifth research question identifies the required data and validation procedure; the original question about predictive performance remains unanswered. This work is retained as a foundation for future research rather than a demonstrated benefit of FamilyFunds.

Testing provided strong local evidence while also revealing the difference between implementation quality and complete operational validation. The canonical local gate passed 1,421 Pest tests with 6,904 assertions and 100.0% PHP coverage, alongside the static-analysis, formatting, lint and client and server-side-rendering build checks reported in Chapter Four. The locked predictive package passed 66 Python tests with 100% statement coverage, and focused Laravel, PostgreSQL and Chromium runs exercised the payment-risk boundary. Twenty redacted images from the synthetic development tenant also document the principal running-system journeys. These results support the conventional application and predictive software implementation locally. They do not replace the pending hosted run, live Paystack and messaging evidence, live AI review, recovery exercise, performance measurements or private-data model evaluation. The sixth research question is therefore partially answered rather than fully closed.

### 5.4 Contributions of the Study

The first contribution is a domain-specific interpretation of multi-tenant financial administration for informal family groups. FamilyFunds treats each family as an independent workspace while recognising that one user may participate in several families. This model is more suitable than assigning one permanent global family role to a person, and it shows how membership context can be combined with policies and scoped queries to represent social responsibilities without weakening data isolation.

The second contribution is the explicit modelling of obligations, receipts and allocations as related but different records. This supports partial payments, lump-sum payments, oldest-balance-first settlement, correction and traceable reporting without rewriting historical obligations. In a family setting, that clarity is valuable because disagreements often concern not only whether money was paid, but also which period it settled and how the current balance was derived.

The third contribution is the integration of operational modules within one governed workflow. Contribution generation, payment processing, expenses, adjustments, reminders, reports, account security, reconciliation and retention are not isolated demonstrations. They share family context, authorisation and financial records. The later reconciliation extension also illustrates a defensible Agile response to an implementation finding: external bank evidence can be compared with internal records while authoritative links remain controlled and auditable.

The fourth contribution is the integration of controlled AI assistance within the existing application permissions. Assistant tools use family-scoped records, and proposed writes require explicit confirmation. The automated tests support those controls, although live response quality was not established. The separate predictive prototype is documented as preparatory engineering work; it is not claimed as a validated prediction method or an improvement in payment behaviour.

Finally, the report contributes an evidence-oriented method for evaluating a student software project. Static implementation evidence, fresh executed evidence and external evidence are reported separately. This makes the findings reproducible and reduces the temptation to treat the existence of code, an earlier continuous-integration run or a synthetic demonstration as proof of current real-world behaviour.

### 5.5 Conclusion

The study concludes that a multi-tenant web application can provide a clearer and more accountable way to administer informal family contribution funds than fragmented notebooks, spreadsheets and messaging conversations. FamilyFunds brings member roles, contribution expectations, payment allocation, expenditure, adjustments and reporting into a shared but family-isolated system. Its deterministic allocation rule and immutable financial history address common sources of uncertainty, while authentication, policies and tenant-aware validation strengthen access control.

The recorded local tests and synthetic-tenant screenshots support the implementation of the main software workflows. FamilyFunds is therefore described as an AI-enhanced multi-tenant family fund management system, with AI enhancement referring to its controlled assistant and report-summary integration. Hosted operation, live integrations, recovery and performance still require further verification, and live AI response quality was not evaluated. Predictive analytics is excluded from the demonstrated outcomes: the project produced a prototype, but training and validation on authorised historical records remain future work. This revised scope reflects the defence feedback without upgrading any incomplete objective to an achieved result.

### 5.6 Limitations

The predictive extension is limited by the absence of an authorised historical dataset. The project could test schema validation, feature reconstruction, training code and scoring calculations, but it could not establish predictive performance on genuine member-period records. The original empirical comparison in Research Question Five was not completed. This limitation does not prevent the main application from recording payments, calculating balances or producing financial reports.

A second limitation is the boundary of the final execution evidence. Although the redacted screenshot register was completed locally, the hosted continuous-integration revision, performance workload, concurrency test, backup recovery exercise and final live-provider checks were not executed. Local automated and interface success offers confidence in the implementation but cannot prove infrastructure health, production configuration or end-to-end delivery through third-party services.

The live usefulness of the AI assistant and report summaries is also unresolved. Role restrictions and confirm-before-write behaviour were tested, but generated responses were not assessed against a live provider for factual correctness, ambiguity, prompt injection, privacy and latency. In addition, the study did not conduct a formal user study with several family groups, so conclusions about ease of use and long-term adoption are based on design and browser checks rather than longitudinal field evidence.

Finally, the system depends on internet access and external services such as Paystack and configured communication providers. Its current payment design is centred on the supported Nigerian context, and the project does not attempt full double-entry accounting, direct bank integration, cryptocurrency support or operation at the scale of large regulated cooperatives. These boundaries should be considered when generalising the findings.

### 5.7 Recommendations

First, the project team should complete the declared release-evidence gate before presenting the system as production-ready. This should include a fresh hosted commit with green test and lint workflows, a healthy deployed application, worker and scheduler checks, a controlled backup-and-restore exercise, and a repeat of the redacted browser evidence set against that same hosted revision. The planned 25-, 75- and 250-member performance workloads should also be run with recorded percentiles, query counts, memory use and error totals.

Second, predictive activation should remain disabled until a formally authorised, pseudonymised dataset passes the provenance, maturity, history and class-balance checks. Training and validation should follow the chronological protocol defined in Chapter Three, and the untouched held-out period should be compared with both transparent baselines. Activation should occur only if the declared F1, balanced-accuracy and Brier-score gates are satisfied. The evaluation package, checksum and model version should be retained for audit, while the private source data and deployable artefact remain outside the public report.

Third, the controlled AI functions should undergo a supervised live acceptance exercise before wider use. Test prompts should cover factual report questions, tenant and role boundaries, missing data, ambiguous requests, prompt injection, member write denial and officer preview-and-confirm flows. Each generated answer should be checked against authoritative application records. Officers should continue to review proposed actions, and AI output should never replace Laravel authorisation, validation or the financial ledger.

Fourth, operating procedures should accompany the software. Family administrators and financial secretaries need concise guidance on contribution generation, corrections, reversals, reconciliation, report handling and privacy. Access should be reviewed when a member's responsibility changes, expired report links should remain short-lived, and retention and legal-hold rules should be applied consistently. Regular restore drills and reconciliation reviews would turn the implemented audit features into dependable organisational practice.

Fifth, any extension to new countries or larger associations should begin with a requirements and compliance review rather than a simple interface change. Payment gateways, currencies, identity practices, record-retention duties and cooperative regulations vary by jurisdiction. The tenant and policy model can provide a foundation, but financial and operational rules must be validated for the intended setting.

### 5.8 Future Work

Predictive analytics is a future research direction. A study would first need authorised historical records from consenting family groups that meet the specified eligibility requirements. The existing logistic-regression prototype could then be trained and compared with both simple baselines using the same chronological partitions. An existing authorised dataset could be used if suitable; waiting a fixed number of months after deployment does not itself guarantee adequate data. Later work could examine calibration over time, subgroup errors, changing payment patterns and retraining criteria. Any validated output should remain advisory and subject to officer review.

A second direction is a structured field study of usability and adoption. Administrators, financial secretaries and members could complete realistic tasks on mobile and desktop devices, followed by interviews or standard usability measures. This would provide evidence about whether report labels, reminder flows, receipts and AI explanations are understandable to the intended users. It could also guide multilingual support, accessibility improvements and a low-connectivity or progressive-web-application mode.

A third direction is deeper financial interoperability. Additional verified payment gateways, bank-statement formats and carefully governed bank-data integrations could reduce manual reconciliation work. More formal groups may also benefit from an optional double-entry accounting layer and export links to established accounting systems. These additions should retain the present separation between external provider events and authoritative family-owned records.

Further work could also study platform-scale tenancy under sustained load. Larger benchmark populations, multiple concurrent families, queue back-pressure and disaster-recovery scenarios would show where caching, indexing, archival or infrastructure changes become necessary. Such testing would provide a sounder basis for moving from a family-focused deployment to broader community or welfare-group use.
